AI safeguard prototype

How should it feel when AI sets a boundary?

The best safety systems don't just restrict behavior. They give people agency, make boundaries understandable and create a path forward.

In the five years I've spent designing Trust & Safety systems at Twitter and Discord, I've learned that saying no to a user is easy; designing what happens next is the real work. With AI, a boundary can't be the end of the interaction. It has to move the conversation forward. I built this prototype with Claude Code to explore how.

    An AI boundary should feel like a fair hearing, even when the answer is no

    In procedural justice terms (more on that below), a good boundary gives the person four things, and each has a lever in the builder:

    • Voice: a chance to say what they actually need. Use a clarifying question and a way forward.
    • Neutrality: a limit that's stated plainly, with a reason. Use firmness and explanation.
    • Respect: a tone that treats them as a capable adult. Use tone.
    • Trust: a sense that the AI cares what they're trying to do. Use a way forward and explanation.

    When it gets something wrong, the same four apply, with ownership taking the place of firmness.

    The interaction is new. The messy human behavior definitely isn't. With this exploration, I'm bringing what I've learned designing safety for messy human systems to a new technology.

    These are hypotheses, not findings. The next step is testing them with real people.

    The difference

    On a platform, you appeal. With AI, you just reply.

    When a platform makes a call you disagree with, you can appeal it later, through a separate process. In an AI conversation, your only way to push back is your next message. So the AI's reply has to leave room for one.

    The challenge

    Platforms manage a crowd. AI is a conversation of two.

    On a platform, a safeguard is mostly a verdict about someone's output, made to protect other people. In an AI conversation, it interrupts the person's own job, often protects that same person, and is made by the only party in the room. The design problem shifts from enforcement to continuation. To see what that changes, I compared AI with three platforms I've worked on, looking at what each is for, who needs protecting, and when a safeguard steps in.

    The stakes

    The best safety is invisible, and a heavy-handed safeguard puts that at risk

    People shouldn't have to notice a safeguard. Heavy-handed ones fail people through dead ends, vague reasons and no path back, so when one has to appear, the job is to make it cost as little as possible.

    The approach

    If a safeguard has to appear, it should be rooted in procedural justice

    At Twitter, we used procedural justice as a design principle for how enforcement messages were written. It names four things people need from an authority they may disagree with: voice, neutrality, respect and trust. I used those as the outcomes, then asked which levers move them: firmness, explanation, a way forward and tone, the AI versions of warn, remove and redirect.
